# Workforce Deficits and Domestic Career Realignment Under Proposed Migration Cuts

## 1. Context: Proposed Migration Contractions

Public debate surrounding Australian immigration policy centers on sharply curbing Net Overseas Migration (NOM) to ease pressures on housing availability, rental yields, and public infrastructure. The two primary policy models under discussion represent different degrees of contraction:

| Policy Framework | NOM Target / Mechanism | Primary Levers | Direct Labor Pool Impact |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Australian Labor Party (ALP) | Target:  $\sim 250,000 - 260,000$ (Halving from peak NOM of  $\sim 528,000$  in 2022–23) | • Caps on international student enrolments.• Increased Temporary Skilled Migration Income Threshold (TSMIT to  $\$70,000+$ ).• Heightened English language requirements.• Crackdowns on unregulated vocational colleges. | Immediate contraction of casual, shift-work, and entry-level service labor (students), alongside secondary skilled-labor pipelines. |
| Pauline Hanson’s One Nation (PHON) | Target: Zero Net Migration or  $\le 20,000$  permanent places | • Near-total freeze on permanent migration intake.• Elimination of post-study work rights.• Strict limits on temporary skilled and humanitarian visas.• Restriction of foreign labor entry to isolated critical exemptions. | Structural shutdown of external labor inflow across primary industries, heavy civil construction, aged care, and regional agriculture. |

Both frameworks rest on an unstated assumption: that a reduction in migrant labor will either induce the domestic population to fill the resulting vacancies or compel industry to automate and lift real wages.

Evaluating this premise requires identifying the sectors most dependent on overseas-born labor and examining the domestic career transitions required to avert systemic supply bottlenecks.

## 2. Sectors Facing Critical Labor Deficits

Migrant workers in Australia are heavily concentrated in foundational, physically demanding, or shift-dependent roles. A severe contraction in overseas intake creates acute labor vacuums in four primary sectors:

### A. The Care Economy (Aged Care, Disability Support, Nursing)

- **Demographic Reliance:** Over  $35\%$  of Australia’s aged care workforce and approximately  $37\%$  of personal care assistants were born overseas.
- **Structural Shortfall:** Australia's population is rapidly aging. The Committee for Economic Development of Australia (CEDA) estimates an annual deficit of at least  $20,000$  to  $25,000$  direct care workers over the coming decade to meet minimum statutory care minutes.
- **Operational Reality:** These roles involve non-standard shifts, physical handling of patients, high burnout rates, and exposure to bodily fluids. Domestic recruitment has persistently failed to meet demand despite recent government-funded award wage increases ( $15\%$ ).

### B. Civil and Residential Construction

- **Demographic Reliance:** Roughly  $24\%$  of the national construction workforce is overseas-born.
- **Structural Shortfall:** To meet the National Housing Accord target of  $1.2\text{ million}$  homes by 2029, industry assessments (BuildSkills Australia, Housing Industry Association) project a deficit exceeding  $90,000$  to  $116,000$  trade and construction workers.
- **Operational Reality:** Heavy structural trades—bricklayers, formwork carpenters, concreters, steel fixers, and roof tilers—suffer domestic apprentice completion dropouts exceeding  $50\%$ . The work is physically taxing, outdoors, and geographically dispersed.

### C. Agriculture, Regional Processing, and Abattoirs

- **Demographic Reliance:** Relies heavily on the Pacific Australia Labour Mobility (PALM) scheme, working holiday makers (backpackers), and sponsored regional visas. In meat processing, overseas workers constitute upwards of  $30\% - 50\%$  of floor labor in regional facilities.
- **Operational Reality:** Located outside major capital cities, these jobs demand high-cadence manual labor, temperature extremes (cold storage or direct summer sun), and repetitive physical tasks. Domestic regional populations are insufficient to satisfy harvest and processing peaks.

### D. Logistics, Sanitation, and Commercial Food Operations

- **Demographic Reliance:** Student visa holders and temporary graduates represent the core staffing buffer for night-shift distribution hubs, commercial cleaning, kitchen hands, and line cooks.
- **Operational Reality:** Operates during anti-social hours (00:00 to 06:00), carries low social prestige, and offers limited upward career mobility.

## 3. The Domestic Labor Distortion: Oversaturated Sectors

While manual, regional, and care sectors face chronic worker deficits, the domestic tertiary-educated workforce is concentrated in white-collar corporate support, administrative management, and tertiary degree credentials:

Australia’s higher education system produces significant annual cohorts in:

- **Generalist Business & Commerce:** Generic management, corporate affairs, entry-level financial analysts.
- **Marketing, Public Relations, and Corporate Communications:** Roles centered around brand strategy, internal messaging, and social media coordination.
- **Human Resources & DEI Administration:** Process-oriented corporate compliance, recruitment coordination, and internal people-management roles.
- **Junior Strategy & Management Consulting:** Process optimization, advisory, and report-writing functions that have faced substantial corporate downsizing.

These roles share common characteristics: they are climate-controlled, desk-bound, digitally mediated, and concentrated in the central business districts of Sydney, Melbourne, and Brisbane.

### 1. The Cost-Push Wage Spiral in Public Services

In sectors like aged care, early childhood education, and disability support (NDIS), wages are funded through government subsidies or federal tax revenues. Doubling or tripling base wages to entice marketing directors into personal care would require:

- Massive tax increases on the remaining working population; or
- Severe rationing of care, leaving elderly Australians without available beds and forcing working-age family members out of the workforce to provide unpaid home care.

### 2. Supply Collapse Outweighs Demand Drop

In housing, halting migration reduces future rental demand increments. However, because civil trades lose a substantial share of their workforce:

- Time to completion expands from 9–12 months to 24+ months.
- The domestic cost per square meter rises due to extreme subcontractor bidding wars.
- Net home completions drop faster than demand declines, preserving or worsening structural supply deficits.

### 3. Structural Resistance to Downward Occupational Mobility

Modern advanced economies exhibit high downward occupational rigidity. Tertiary-educated workers in oversaturated professions generally do not transition into manual trades or sanitation work when white-collar jobs dry up; instead, they experience:

- Extended periods of underemployment (part-time retail, hospitality, or rideshare driving);
- Prolonged reliance on social welfare systems; or
- "Credential inflation," where masters degrees are pursued to compete for entry-level administrative roles.

## 6. Analytical Conclusion

The domestic debate surrounding migration targets presents a fundamental contradiction. Proponents of near-zero intake advocate for the termination of external labor supply while expecting the continuation of cheap food distribution, expanding healthcare entitlements, functional municipal infrastructure, and rapid home building.

Without high inward migration in vocational, care, and primary production segments, the only mathematical mechanism to balance the labor ledger is a state-directed, large-scale redeployment of domestic capital and labor.

Demanding an end to migration while maintaining an urban economy dominated by clerical, strategic, and digital marketing roles is an untenable position. The actual price of complete immigration restriction is not merely a smaller population; it is the physical reallocation of the domestic workforce into the demanding, dirty, and essential occupations currently sustained by overseas workers.
