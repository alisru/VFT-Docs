The political establishment may be underestimating one of the biggest stories unfolding in Australian politics. According to polling discussed on Jeremy Cordeaux’s Court of Public Opinion, Pauline Hanson and One Nation are experiencing a resurgence that few predicted after the federal election. The numbers being floated are extraordinary — Cordeaux cited figures suggesting Hanson could win as many as sixty-three seats if a federal election were held today. Whether that figure holds in coming months or not, the trend is unmistakable.
While Labor celebrates its electoral dominance and the Coalition searches for direction, a growing number of Australians appear to be looking elsewhere. For the political class, the question is no longer whether One Nation is relevant. It is how relevant it might become.
Cost of living pressures continue to dominate family budgets across Australia. Mortgage holders remain under pressure as interest rates stay stubbornly high. Grocery bills remain elevated despite official inflation figures suggesting moderation. Energy prices continue to frustrate households, particularly through the winter months when usage spikes.
Advertisement
For many Australians, neither Labor nor the Coalition appears to be offering solutions that inspire genuine confidence. Both major parties are increasingly perceived as managing decline rather than reversing it. That frustration creates fertile ground for minor parties and independents — and Hanson, who has spent decades positioning herself as an outsider, is uniquely placed to harvest it.
Pauline Hanson has always positioned herself as someone willing to challenge political orthodoxy. Supporters argue she speaks directly about immigration, government spending, bureaucracy and economic management without relying on carefully scripted political language.
Whether voters agree with every One Nation policy is almost secondary. Many simply appreciate hearing concerns they believe are routinely ignored by Canberra. In a political environment dominated by managed messaging and focus-group-tested talking points, the perceived authenticity is itself the selling point.
The biggest threat posed by One Nation is not necessarily to Labor. Historically, One Nation has drawn significant support from conservative and regional voters who might otherwise support Liberal or National candidates.
Advertisement
If current polling trends continue, the Coalition could face increasing pressure to redefine its message and reconnect with voters concerned about economic security, government accountability, and the basic competence of public institutions. The risk for the Coalition is that it gets squeezed between a Labor government in power and an insurgent right-wing force on its conservative flank.
Cordeaux highlighted another dimension worth watching. Mainstream media outlets, particularly the ABC, have come under criticism for what some perceive as defensive coverage of Hanson’s resurgence. Whether or not those concerns are justified, the cultural conversation around her is shifting. Topics once dismissed as fringe — immigration intake levels, public service growth, union influence — are increasingly being discussed openly across the political spectrum.
Australian politics has entered a period of genuine volatility. The traditional two-party system is under pressure from all directions. Minor parties continue gaining influence. Voter loyalty is weakening. Trust in institutions remains fragile.
If Pauline Hanson continues building momentum, the next election could become one of the most unpredictable contests in modern Australian history. The political class may not like it. But many Australians appear ready to send a message — and they are looking for someone willing to carry it.
