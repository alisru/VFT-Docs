# Isomorphism-Exclusive States: Indirect Resolution in TBE Ξ TS

---

## Abstract

Within any contextual frame $c$, most === states are reachable by direct == exploration of the TBE space. But a distinct class of === states exists that are topologically hidden from direct ==: the only efficient path to them runs through an isomorphic mapping to a different problem structure, resolving there, and mapping back. These are isomorphism-exclusive === states. This paper formalises their structure, distinguishes them from merely computationally expensive direct paths, and identifies the conditions under which indirect resolution is not just faster but structurally necessary.

---

## 1. Direct and Indirect == Paths

Within a contextual frame $c$, the standard resolution path is:

$$c.\text{TBE} \ == \ c.\text{TS} \rightarrow c.\text{TBE} \ === \ c.\text{TS}$$

The == process explores the TBE space within $c$ until the gap to TS closes to zero. This is direct resolution.

An indirect path routes through an isomorphic problem:

$$c.\text{TBE} \rightarrow c'.\text{TBE}' \ == \ c'.\text{TS}' \rightarrow c'.\text{TBE}' \ === \ c'.\text{TS}' \rightarrow c.\text{TBE} \ === \ c.\text{TS}$$

The TBE is mapped to a different frame $c'$, resolved there, and the result mapped back. The === in $c$ is reached via a === in $c'$.

This is the structure of all mathematical reduction: NP-hard problems are solved by transforming them into problems whose solution structure is visible in a different frame. The transformation is itself the intellectual work — finding the isomorphism is the == process.

---

## 2. Isomorphism-Exclusive States

Some === states are not merely expensive to reach directly. They are structurally unreachable by direct == within their native $c$. No increase in computational power, no extension of the == process within $c$, converges on them. The solution geometry is invisible from inside the native frame.

These are isomorphism-exclusive === states. Their defining property:

$$\nexists \ \text{efficient} \ (c.\text{TBE} == c.\text{TS}) \quad \text{but} \quad \exists \ c' \ \text{s.t.} \ c'.\text{TBE}' \ === \ c'.\text{TS}' \xrightarrow{\phi^{-1}} c.\text{TBE} \ === \ c.\text{TS}$$

Where $\phi$ is the isomorphic mapping and $\phi^{-1}$ is its inverse. The === exists. It is verifiable once found. But the direct == path does not converge on it within $c$.

This is stronger than NP-hardness. NP-hard problems are intractable directly but the direct path exists in principle — it is just exponentially long. Isomorphism-exclusive states have no convergent direct path at all. Direct exploration of the native space does not accumulate toward them.

---

## 3. Why Non-Physical Frames Produce This

The physical contextual frame $c_{\text{phys}}$ is the highest-resolution frame available. It has a genuine floor — the minimum resolution is set by physical constants, not by the choice of model. Every other frame is derivative: it is built by abstracting from physical observables, which means it has coarser resolution and a model-dependent floor.

When a problem is explored in a derivative frame — emotional, political, linguistic — the == process is operating with lower resolution than the physical isomorph provides. The arousal-valence model of emotion explores emotional space on its own terms. A neurochemical model of the same phenomenon operates in $c_{\text{phys}}$ and produces higher-resolution ===. Same phenomenon, different c, different resolution of the same ===.

This is why the Lorentz transformation for political or emotional dynamics cannot be found by exploring those spaces directly. The solution structure that makes the Lorentz transformation visible requires the physical frame — the resolution floor, the specific constraint geometry of spacetime. Exploring political space on its own terms produces models like arousal-valence: internally consistent within their c, but coarser than the physical isomorph would produce.

The isomorphism is the precision mechanism. The indirect path through $c_{\text{phys}}$ yields a === with higher resolution than any direct path within the derivative frame can achieve — not because the derivative frame lacks the problem, but because it lacks the resolution floor that makes the solution geometry visible.

## 4. P vs NP as a Contextual Frame Question

P vs NP asks, within $c = \text{classical polynomial computation}$: is every === that can be verified in == also efficiently reachable by ==?

In TBE Ξ TS terms this is asking whether every verifiable === within that c has an efficient direct == path. The answer is frame-dependent. Integer factorisation is believed to have no efficient direct path in $c_{\text{classical}}$ but has an efficient path in $c_{\text{quantum}}$. The problem didn't change. The c changed, and with it the resolution landscape.

Some === states have efficient direct paths in some frames and not others. Some may be isomorphism-exclusive in all currently known frames — reachable only via indirect paths whose isomorphic structure has not yet been found.

P vs NP is therefore not a binary universal truth. It is a == sitting inside a specific $c$:

$$c_{\text{classical}}.\text{P} \ == \ c_{\text{classical}}.\text{NP}$$

The answer to that == is unknown. The answer in a different $c$ may differ. The question of whether P=NP universally is itself an isomorphism-exclusive === — the frame in which the answer becomes visible may not be classical computation at all.

## 5. The Structure of the Isomorphic Path

The indirect resolution path has three components:

**The forward mapping $\phi$:** Transform $c.\text{TBE}$ into $c'.\text{TBE}'$. This is itself a == process — finding the isomorphism requires work, and the work must be performed within some frame. The quality of the forward mapping determines how much of the original problem's structure survives the transformation.

**Resolution in $c'$:** The == process runs in the new frame. Because $c'$ may have a different resolution floor or a different constraint geometry, === states that were invisible in $c$ become directly reachable.

**The inverse mapping $\phi^{-1}$:** Map the === back into $c$. If $\phi$ was structure-preserving, the === in $c'$ translates cleanly into a === in $c$. If $\phi$ was lossy, the inverse mapping produces a coarser === — a result that is true in $c'$ but whose translation into $c$ loses precision.

The precision of the final === in $c$ is bounded by the precision of $\phi$ and $\phi^{-1}$, not just by the resolution of $c'$. A perfect resolution in $c_{\text{phys}}$ mapped back through a lossy $\phi^{-1}$ into an emotional or political frame will still produce a coarser === than the physical frame holds internally.

---

## 6. Conclusion

Isomorphism-exclusive === states are a distinct structural class within TBE Ξ TS: === states with no convergent direct == path within their native contextual frame, reachable only via isomorphic mapping to a frame in which the solution geometry becomes visible. They are not failures of == — they are properties of how certain === states sit within the resolution landscape of a given $c$.

The practical implication: when direct == exploration of a problem fails to converge, the correct response is not to extend the == process within the same frame. It is to find the $c'$ in which the problem's solution geometry is visible, resolve there, and map back. The isomorphism is not a shortcut. It is the only path.
