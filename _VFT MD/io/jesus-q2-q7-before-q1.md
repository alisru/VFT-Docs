# Jesus — Q2 Through Q7 Before Q1
### The Six Planes First. The WHO Emerges Last.

---

## Q2 — WHAT (The Possible Plane)
### What actually happened? What was claimed? What is the textual record?

**W1. A Galilean Jewish preacher who proclaimed the imminent Kingdom of God**
Mark 1:14-15: "The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand."
Josephus, Antiquities 18.3.3 (Testimonium Flavianum, partial): confirms execution under Pilate.
Albert Schweitzer, The Quest of the Historical Jesus (1906): argued Jesus was an apocalyptic prophet who expected the end within his own generation.

**W2. A healer and exorcist operating within Jewish practice**
Mark 1:23-28 (exorcism in synagogue), Mark 5:1-20 (Gerasene demoniac), Luke 7:22 / Matthew 11:5 (Q source): "the blind see, the lame walk, lepers are cleansed."
John Meier, A Marginal Jew, Vol. 2 (1994): concluded that healings and exorcisms are among the most historically secure data points about Jesus, assessed by criteria of multiple attestation and embarrassment.

**W3. A wisdom teacher working in the Cynic-Stoic tradition**
Thomas 47, 54, 63-65 (Gospel of Thomas); Luke 6:20-26 (Beatitudes in Q form).
John Dominic Crossan, The Historical Jesus (1991): argued Jesus operated as an itinerant Cynic sage, distributing healing and table fellowship against the purity economy.
Burton Mack, A Myth of Innocence (1988): read Mark as myth-making imposed on a wisdom teacher.

**W4. A prophet self-consciously in the line of Isaiah**
Luke 4:18-19: Jesus reads Isaiah 61:1-2 in the Nazareth synagogue and declares "Today this scripture is fulfilled."
N.T. Wright, Jesus and the Victory of God (1996): argued Jesus understood himself as the climax of Israel's prophetic narrative, embodying Israel's vocation.

**W5. A claimant to Messianic authority over Israel**
Mark 14:61-62: high priest asks "Are you the Christ?" Jesus replies "I am."
Matthew 16:16-17: Peter's confession at Caesarea Philippi, affirmed by Jesus.
Multiple messianic action-signals: triumphal entry (Mark 11:1-10), Temple action (Mark 11:15-18), Last Supper covenant language (Mark 14:22-25).

**W6. A figure making unique divine sonship claims**
John 10:30: "I and the Father are one."
John 8:58: "Before Abraham was, I am" — direct invocation of Exodus 3:14.
John 17 (High Priestly Prayer): pre-existent glory claimed.
Note: Johannine Christology is higher and later than synoptic — critical scholars treat these as theological development, not ipsissima verba.

**W7. A teacher who left almost no verified written trace and whose words were transmitted orally for decades**
Paul's letters (~49-60 CE) predate the earliest gospel (Mark, ~70 CE) by 10-20 years.
The Q source (reconstructed): a sayings collection used by Matthew and Luke independently, possibly earlier than Mark.
Robert Funk et al., The Five Gospels (1993) / Jesus Seminar: rated only ~18% of sayings as likely authentic by strict criteria.

## Q3 — WHERE (The Physical Plane)
### The material, geographic, social, and political conditions.

**Wh1. Roman-occupied Galilee under Herod Antipas's tetrarchy**
Luke 3:1: Tiberius Caesar, Pontius Pilate, Herod, Philip, Lysanias all contemporaneous.
Galilee was administered separately from Judea; a relatively prosperous agricultural region but under tribute extraction.
Richard Horsley, Jesus and the Spiral of Violence (1987): modeled Roman tribute as the structural driver of peasant debt and displacement — Jesus's audience.

**Wh2. Nazareth — a village of ~400 people, no mention in any pre-Christian source**
John 1:46: "Can anything good come from Nazareth?"
Archaeological record: Nazareth confirmed by 1st-century tomb finds; not mentioned in Josephus or Talmud despite listing nearby towns.
Jonathan Reed, Archaeology and the Galilean Jesus (2000): recovered material culture consistent with conservative Jewish village identity.

**Wh3. Bethlehem birth — theologically motivated, historically uncertain**
Matthew 2 (Herod, Magi, flight to Egypt) and Luke 2 (census, manger) are irreconcilable in detail.
The census of Quirinius (Luke 2:2) is dated to 6 CE — 10 years after Herod's death.
Raymond Brown, The Birth of the Messiah (1977): concluded both infancy narratives are theological midrash, not historical reporting.

**Wh4. Death by crucifixion outside Jerusalem under Pontius Pilate**
Mark 15:22-37; confirmed independently by Tacitus, Annals 15.44 (c. 116 CE): "Christus...suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of...Pontius Pilate."
Josephus, Antiquities 18.3.3 (core): "Pilate...condemned him to be crucified."
Crucifixion was Roman state execution reserved for slaves, insurrectionists, and threats to public order — not a Jewish penalty.

**Wh5. The Temple as political and economic centre of gravity**
The Jerusalem Temple functioned as a bank, tax collection node, and symbol of covenantal identity simultaneously.
E.P. Sanders, Jesus and Judaism (1985): argued the Temple action (Mark 11:15-18) was the proximate cause of Jesus's arrest — a symbolic prophetic act against the priestly establishment, not a cleansing.
Josephus, Jewish War: documents the Temple's enormous economic power and the volatile politics of the period.

**Wh6. A world of high infant mortality, peasant subsistence, and regular famine**
John J. Rousseau & Rami Arav, Jesus and His World (1995): archaeological and sociological reconstruction.
Dominic Crossan and Jonathan Reed, Excavating Jesus (2001): distinguished between Hellenised cities (Sepphoris, Tiberias) and conservative Jewish villages — Jesus operated in the latter circuit.

## Q4 — WHY (The Lyrical Plane)
### What meaning was being carried? For whom? Why these words and these acts?

**Wh4-1. The Kingdom of God as anti-imperial counter-world**
Mark 1:15; Luke 17:20-21.
N.T. Wright, The New Testament and the People of God (1992): "Kingdom of God" in Jewish ears meant the end of pagan domination and restoration of covenant — a political claim as much as a spiritual one.
Richard Horsley: the Kingdom proclamation was addressed specifically to displaced peasants and carried implicit judgment on the Herodian client-king arrangement.

**Wh4-2. Table fellowship as enacted theology — the boundary violation as the message**
Mark 2:15-17: eating with tax collectors and sinners.
Luke 15:1-2; Luke 19:1-10 (Zacchaeus).
Marcus Borg, Meeting Jesus Again for the First Time (1994): argued table sharing was the central symbolic act — crossing purity boundaries publicly meant claiming a different social order.
Crossan: open commensality was the method. Anyone ate with anyone. That is the sign.

**Wh4-3. Subversion of honour-shame culture toward a different valuation of persons**
Luke 14:7-14: do not take the seat of honour; invite those who cannot repay you.
Matthew 5:3-12 (Beatitudes): systematic inversion of honour-categories — blessed are the poor, the mourning, the meek.
Kenneth Bailey, Poet and Peasant (1976): read the parables through Middle Eastern village social logic — the prodigal son (Luke 15:11-32) as a sustained inversion of honour and shame categories.

**Wh4-4. Fulfillment-as-intensification of Torah, not abolition**
Matthew 5:17-48: "You have heard it was said...but I say unto you." Six antitheses — each goes deeper than the letter.
James D.G. Dunn, Jesus Remembered (2003): Jesus was working within the covenant renewal tradition, radicalising Torah from the inside.

**Wh4-5. A personal claim of unique intimacy with God as Father**
Mark 14:36: "Abba, Father" — Aramaic address preserved in Greek text, suggesting authentic early memory.
Jeremias, The Prayers of Jesus (1967): argued "Abba" as address to God was unprecedented in Judaism — a claim to a unique filial relationship.
This WHY, if true, would carry its own gravitational field regardless of tradition.

**Wh4-6. The address to Israel specifically — not a universal mission in his own lifetime**
Matthew 10:5-6: "Do not go to the Gentiles...go rather to the lost sheep of Israel."
Matthew 15:24: "I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel."
N.T. Wright: Jesus saw himself as calling Israel back to her covenant vocation so that through Israel the nations would be drawn in. Universal scope was downstream, not immediate.

## Q5 — HOW (The Logical Plane)
### Internal coherence. Source structure. How do the claims hold under load?

**H1. The synoptic problem — Mark written first, Matthew and Luke used Mark plus Q**
The two-source hypothesis: Mark (~70 CE) + Q (hypothetical sayings source) = Matthew and Luke independently.
Matthew and Luke each add material unique to themselves (M and L sources respectively).
Kloppenborg, The Formation of Q (1987): Q may have existed in layers, the earliest being a wisdom collection without a passion narrative.

**H2. Paul's creed predates any gospel by 20+ years**
1 Corinthians 15:3-8 (~54 CE) contains a pre-Pauline formula ("I received...I passed on") — Paul received it within a few years of the crucifixion (~35 CE).
This creed lists resurrection appearances: Peter, the Twelve, 500 brethren, James, all the apostles, Paul.
This is the closest evidence to the events — no passion narrative, no empty tomb, pure testimony of appearances.

**H3. The criteria of historical authenticity**
Multiple attestation: a saying or event appearing in independent sources (Mark, Q, Paul, John, Thomas) is more likely authentic.
Criterion of embarrassment: material awkward for the early church (Jesus's baptism by John, cry of dereliction, family opposition) is unlikely to have been invented.
Criterion of discontinuity: sayings unlike both Judaism and early Christianity are more likely to originate with Jesus himself.
John Meier applied all three systematically across four volumes.

**H4. The empty tomb — independent attestation problem**
Paul (1 Cor 15) does not mention an empty tomb explicitly.
Mark 16:1-8 ends without appearances — the women flee in fear (earliest manuscripts end at 16:8).
Matthew 28:11-15 records the counter-tradition: the body was stolen.
The tomb tradition is early, local, and specific (named women witnesses). N.T. Wright, The Resurrection of the Son of God (2003): argued that neither hallucination theory nor theft accounts for the specific Jewish content of "resurrection" as distinct from other survival categories.

**H5. The coherence problem between John and the Synoptics**
John's timeline: three Passovers (a 3-year ministry); Last Supper is not a Passover meal.
Synoptic timeline: one Passover (a 1-year or shorter ministry); Last Supper is explicitly a Passover seder.
John's Jesus speaks in long theological discourses; synoptic Jesus speaks in short aphorisms and parables — almost no overlap.
Rudolf Bultmann, Theology of the New Testament (1951): John represents a thoroughgoing theological interpretation, not independent historical memory.

**H6. The Q material's internal coherence**
The Sermon on the Mount/Plain material (Matthew 5-7 / Luke 6:20-49) has strong internal coherence as a unified ethical teaching.
The "double tradition" material (present in both Matthew and Luke, absent in Mark) includes some of the most striking sayings: Lord's Prayer, the mission charge, the golden rule, the narrow gate.
Its absence of a resurrection narrative or passion prediction may indicate an early form that pre-dates those theological developments.

## Q6 — CAUSE (The Historical Plane)
### What prior sequence produced this person and this event?

**C1. Second Temple Judaism's multiple messianic trajectories**
Dead Sea Scrolls (4Q521): "The Messiah" will heal the sick and raise the dead — language that maps directly onto Q's report of Jesus's deeds (Luke 7:22).
1 Enoch (1st century BCE): "Son of Man" as heavenly judge figure — the title Jesus uses most frequently of himself.
Isaiah 61:1-2 (6th century BCE): the anointed one who brings good news to the poor — the text Jesus reads in Nazareth.
Multiple messianic templates were live in 1st-century Jewish expectation — Jesus activated several simultaneously.

**C2. John the Baptist as the immediate structural precursor**
Mark 1:1-11; Matthew 3; Luke 3.
Josephus, Antiquities 18.5.2: confirms John as a real historical figure, "a good man" who "commanded the Jews to exercise virtue."
John's baptism of Jesus is certain (criterion of embarrassment — the early church would not have invented the superior baptising the inferior).
The Baptist movement created the frame: repentance, water, imminent judgment.

**C3. The Deuteronomic prophetic tradition — suffering prophets who are vindicated**
1 Kings 19 (Elijah); Jeremiah 37-38 (imprisoned prophet); Isaiah 52:13-53:12 (Suffering Servant).
Luke 4:25-27: Jesus explicitly invokes Elijah and Elisha as parallels to his own ministry.
The pattern of rejected-then-vindicated prophet was an available interpretive frame — early followers used it to make sense of the crucifixion immediately.

**C4. Maccabean martyrdom theology — suffering as redemptive**
2 Maccabees 7 (2nd century BCE): the seven brothers tortured by Antiochus — their deaths are described as atoning for the nation.
4 Maccabees develops this further.
N.T. Wright: the first followers reaching for "his death was for our sins" (1 Cor 15:3) were drawing on a pre-existing theological grammar.

**C5. Roman imperial theology as structural antagonist**
Augustus was called "Son of God," "Lord," "Saviour," bringer of "peace" (pax romana) through conquest.
Neil Elliott, Liberating Paul (1994): the early Jesus movement's titles for Jesus precisely mirror and invert the imperial title-system.
The announcement "Jesus is Lord" (Kyrios Iesous) was a direct counter-proclamation to "Caesar is Lord" (Kyrios Kaiser).

**C6. Hellenistic Judaism's synthesis — the Logos tradition**
Philo of Alexandria (20 BCE - 50 CE): developed the concept of the Logos (divine Word/Reason) as the mediating principle between God and creation.
John 1:1-14 uses Logos vocabulary directly — "In the beginning was the Logos."
The prologue of John reads as a translation of the Jesus event into Hellenistic Jewish philosophical categories already in circulation.

**C7. The immediate social displacement context — Galilean debt crisis**
Horsley: Herod Antipas's building projects (Sepphoris, Tiberias) were funded by increased tribute extraction from Galilean peasants.
The parables of debt (Matthew 18:23-35, Luke 16:1-9) and the Lord's Prayer "forgive us our debts" (Matthew 6:12) map directly onto this material condition.
The audience for Jesus's message was structurally produced by Roman-Herodian economic extraction.

## Q7 — EFFECT (The Emotive Plane)
### What actually happened to people who encountered this? What are the downstream consequences?

**E1. Immediate scattering — then unexpected cohesion**
Mark 14:50: at the arrest "they all deserted him and fled."
The disciples' regrouping around resurrection experiences within weeks is historically agreed by virtually all scholars — the disagreement is about what caused it.
N.T. Wright: the combination of empty tomb + appearances is the only explanation that accounts for the specific Jewish content of the resurrection claim.
Gerd Lüdemann, The Resurrection of Jesus (1994): argued for grief-induced hallucinations originating with Peter.

**E2. Communities forming across the Roman Empire within decades**
Paul's letters (49-60 CE): churches in Galatia, Corinth, Philippi, Thessalonica, Rome.
Pliny the Younger, Letter to Trajan (c. 112 CE): describes Christians in Bithynia meeting before dawn, singing hymns "to Christ as to a god."
Acts of the Apostles (c. 80-90 CE): narrative of rapid geographic expansion — Jerusalem, Judea, Samaria, Asia Minor, Greece, Rome.

**E3. A crucified criminal becoming an object of worship — unique in the ancient world**
No other crucified person was worshipped as divine in the ancient Mediterranean.
Martin Hengel, Crucifixion (1977): documented that crucifixion was considered the most degrading death possible — "the slaves' punishment." The claim that such a person was Lord and God was not persuasive; it was scandalous.
Paul, 1 Corinthians 1:23: "We preach Christ crucified: a stumbling block to Jews and foolishness to Gentiles."

**E4. Transformation of the Roman Empire**
Edict of Milan (313 CE): Constantine and Licinius grant religious tolerance.
Theodosius I (380 CE): Christianity becomes the state religion of Rome.
A movement originating in a peasant province became the ideological foundation of the empire that had executed its founder within 350 years.

**E5. Liberation movements drawing directly on the tradition**
Abolitionism: Frederick Douglass, William Wilberforce — both explicitly Christian.
Civil Rights Movement: Martin Luther King Jr., James Lawson — rooted in Black church theology of the suffering-servant.
James Cone, The Cross and the Lynching Tree (2011): argued that the cross as a symbol of state execution of an innocent person was the most direct analogue to the lynching of Black Americans — and that seeing it this way is what the tradition actually requires.

**E6. Institutional religion weaponising the tradition against its apparent content**
The Crusades (1095-1291): military campaigns justified in Jesus's name.
The Inquisition (1184-1834): torture and execution under Church authority.
Colonial mission (16th-20th century): mass conversion by coercion throughout Africa, the Americas, Asia.
The structural Q7 irony: a tradition originating in opposition to imperial violence became the ideological instrument of empire repeatedly.

**E7. Personal transformation reports — cross-cultural and trans-historical**
William James, The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902): documented cases of conversion, mystical experience, and moral transformation attributed to encounter with the Jesus tradition.
These reports are distributed across all cultures, classes, and centuries — not confined to any ethnic or social group.
They constitute data at Q7 regardless of what Q1 turns out to be.

**E8. The canonical Gospels as literature — their effect on language and culture**
The Authorised Version (1611) shaped the English language structurally.
Dostoevsky, Tolstoy, Flannery O'Connor, Graham Greene — literary traditions inseparable from the Gospel narratives.
Northrop Frye, The Great Code (1982): argued the Bible is the structural master narrative underlying Western literary production.

## Q1 — WHO (The Emergent Axis)
### What WHO answers are defensible after running Q2 through Q7?

These are not competing answers to choose between.
They are legitimate emergent identities at different depths and from different evidential starting points.
Any Q1 claim that was not earned by running the above is not a finding — it is a flag planted in open ground.

**Q1-A. An apocalyptic Jewish prophet who expected the end within his generation**
Earned by: W1, Wh1, Wh5, C1, C2, H1.
The end did not come. This WHO predicts a failed prophet — or a prophet whose failure requires reinterpretation.
Schweitzer's position. Most consequential unresolved tension in historical Jesus scholarship.

**Q1-B. A Cynic-style wisdom sage who generated a distinctive ethical tradition**
Earned by: W3, Wh4-2, Wh4-3, Q Source material, H6.
No apocalyptic urgency required. No unique divine claims required. Transmissible as pure ethical teaching.
Crossan, Mack. Attractive to scholars uncomfortable with supernatural claims.

**Q1-C. A prophet of covenantal renewal within Israel's own narrative**
Earned by: W4, Wh4-1, Wh4-6, C1, C3, C4, H3.
Jesus as Israel-completing rather than Israel-replacing. The death as a prophetic act, not a substitutionary mechanism.
N.T. Wright's position. The most historically grounded theologically conservative reading.

**Q1-D. A social revolutionary whose primary axis was economic and political**
Earned by: Wh1, Wh6, Wh4-2, C5, C7, E5.
The Kingdom as social inversion. The table fellowship as political act. The death as the state's response to a threat.
Horsley, Crossan's political reading, liberation theology tradition.

**Q1-E. The incarnate Logos — divine in nature, human in form**
Earned by: W6, C6, E2, E3, H4 (resurrection evidence taken at face value), E7.
Requires: taking Paul's early creed (H2) seriously, taking the empty tomb tradition seriously, taking the Johannine theological framework as revelatory not merely interpretive.
Nicene orthodoxy. Requires the most from the evidence but is not disqualified by the evidence.

**Q1-F. A psychological archetype — the individuating Self made visible**
Earned by: E7, E8, the cross-cultural resonance data.
Carl Jung, Answer to Job (1952); Joseph Campbell, The Hero with a Thousand Faces (1949).
WHO as structural function in the human psyche rather than a historical or metaphysical claim. The story does what it does regardless of the historical facts beneath it.

**Q1-G. All of the above simultaneously, at different planes**
The Q2-Q7 data does not produce a single clean Q1.
It produces a figure who was:
— historically embedded in a specific time, place, and crisis (Q3/Q6)
— carrying a coherent meaning-structure for a specific audience (Q4)
— generating effects that exceeded what any single Q1 label accounts for (Q7)
— leaving a textual record that resists simple resolution (Q2/Q5)

The Q1 that emerges from the full plane scan is not a neat category.
It is a convergence pressure — something that caused effects at every plane simultaneously.
What that thing is remains the open question.

The only honest position after running Q2-Q7:
the WHO is the hardest question, not the easiest.
Anyone who answers it quickly has not done the work.
