its composition at about the third century after Christ ; 
and perhaps there are really echoes in this Brahmanic 
poem of the lessons of Galilee, and of the Syrian 
incarnation. 

Its scene is the level country between the Jumna and 
the Sarsooti rivers — now Kurnul and Jheend. Its 
simple plot consists of a dialogue held by Prince Arjuna, 
the brother of King Yudhisthira, with Krishna, the 
Supreme Deity, wearing the disguise of a charioteer. 
A great battle is impending between the armies of 
the Kauravas and Pandavas, and this conversation is 
maintained in a war-chariot drawn up between the 
opposing hosts. 

The poem has been turned into French by Burnouf, 
into Latin by Lassen, into Italian by Stanislav Gatti, 
into Greek by Galanos, and into English by Mr. 
Thomson and Mr. Davies, the prose transcript of the 
last-named being truly beyond praise for its fidelity 
and clearness. Mr. Telang has also published at Bom- 
bay a version in colloquial rhythm, eminently learned 
and intelligent, but not conveying the dignity or grace 
of the original. If I venture to offer a translation of 
the wonderful poem after so many superior scholars, it 
is in grateful recognition of the help derived from their 
labors, and because English literature would certainly

There is no writer better qualified by personal experience and observation to 
write a book on the mysterious land of the Orientals. Besides, he had exceptional 
advantages of meeting distinguished people in India who gave him every facility 
of sight-seeing and society, in all directions. His descriptions include many of 
those interesting little every-day Oriental scenes and customs that most travellers 
consider unworthy of mention, but which are necessary to give reality and local 
coloring to their pictures. Mr. Arnold's descriptions are filled with the same 
poetical charm that pervades all his works. The first four chapters devoted to 
the voyage from England to India give a graphic idea of Indian and Anglo-Indian 
life on board the steamer, the maritime sights on the way, and the passage 
through the Suez Canal, which enchains the attention no less than the description 
of the land that follows it. . . . 

There are chapters on famous Eastern jewels, the Nautch girls in their plays 
and dances, the Snake-charmers and the practical reasons given to show why 
they can be so familiar with their frightful playmates. None but a poet could so 
truthfully describe the rose-red city of Jeypore, and the wonderful tomb of the 
Taj, or the " Crown of the World," and its marvellous surroundings. Beneath 
the author's magic touch they arise, stately and splendid, unveiled to the mental 
vision like the heavenly pictures of Revelation. The book is illustrated by 
thirty-two engravings, and also contains a fine dramatic poem of Mr. Arnold's 
hitherto uupublished. — New York Tittees.
